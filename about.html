// About the Founder

The middle schooler who
refused to wait.

Newton, MA. A basement. A soldering iron. A cold email to a stranger at Berkeley. And a system that turned all of it into something real.

// In his own words

How it started.

My older brother wanted to be an engineer. When I was really young, I used to copy everything he did — his projects, his interests, the way he thought about problems. He'd design things and I'd try to design them too. He'd build things and I'd try to build them alongside him.

At some point, his interests moved on. But mine never did. The engineering, the building, the obsession with how things actually work — that just became who I am. While he moved on, I went deeper. I didn't know what I was getting into. I just knew I wasn't done.

I started with an HHO generator. Built it myself, machined custom parts, optimized the output. I was 13. From there it was CAD design — thousands of iterations trying to get a single part right. Then I decided I wanted to build a quantum sensing setup.

Not a kit. Not a simulation. A real, functional ODMR setup — the kind of equipment that normally lives in a university physics lab — in my basement, on less than $2,500.

Everyone who understood what I was attempting thought it was a lot. Maybe too much. I logged over 2,000 CAD iterations on that project. I rebuilt it. I troubleshot it. I hit walls I had no idea how to get past. Eight months in, I detected a Zeeman shift. A real result. An experimental physics result. In my basement.

But here's the part that actually changed everything: I realized I needed teachers. I was teaching myself calculus and AP Physics and I needed people who actually understood them. I had no connections. I had no money for expensive tutors. So I did the only thing I could think of — I emailed strangers.

A Berkeley graduate student. A theoretical physics PhD. I wrote them both cold — no introduction, no connection, no reason they should say yes. They both said yes.

That's when I realized: the system I'd been building — the process, the method for getting unstuck, the way I thought about finding the right people — was actually the most valuable thing I had. More valuable than any single result.

So I wrote it down.

// The Framework

The philosophy.

"Everything has a process. Identify it. Walk through it."
— Sammy, Newton MA

This isn't inspiration. It's a technical observation. Every goal — no matter how complex, how intimidating, how out-of-reach it appears — is reducible to a sequence of steps. The steps may be long. Some of them may be hard. Some of them may require help from people you haven't met yet. But the sequence exists.

The first job is always to find the sequence. Not to be talented enough to skip it — to find it. Most people don't do this. They jump in, hit resistance, interpret resistance as a signal that they're not capable, and stop. The resistance was never evidence of incapacity. It was just a step they hadn't figured out yet.

The second job is to walk through it — one step, then the next. When you hit a wall, you don't abandon the process. You find what's blocking you, isolate it, test your assumptions about it, and move.

I built a quantum sensing setup in my basement by following this logic, step by step, over eight months, through 2,000 iterations of a design that kept not working. I cold emailed strangers who had every reason to ignore me, because the logic said: find the step that gets you unstuck, and take it.

The system works. The proof is the Zeeman shift. The proof is the Berkeley grad student who answered. The proof is the physics PhD who taught me. The proof is the app that's live.

// The Insight

How I figured out cold outreach.

I needed to learn calculus. I didn't have money for a tutor. I didn't know anyone who could teach me. I sat with that for a while, and then I asked myself a simple question: who on earth actually knows this well, and is there any reason they wouldn't help a middle schooler who's genuinely curious?

The answer was: graduate students. They know the material deeply. Many of them actually like teaching it. And a polite, specific, respectful email from a 13-year-old who's building a quantum sensing setup and wants to learn calculus is not something most people say no to.

So I wrote the email. I didn't lead with an ask. I introduced myself, described what I was working on, explained what I was trying to learn, and left the door open. The graduate student at Berkeley responded. He agreed to teach me.

I did the same thing for AP Physics. A theoretical physics PhD this time. Same approach. Same result.

What I learned from this is something nobody tells teenagers: most experts — researchers, PhDs, graduate students — are not inaccessible. They're just rarely asked in the right way. The barrier is not status. The barrier is not connections. The barrier is not money. The barrier is knowing how to make an ask that costs the other person nothing to say yes to.

That's the framework. And it's learnable.

// On Failure

2,000 iterations.

"I didn't solve the problem by being smarter than it. I solved it by refusing to stop."

The ODMR setup took 8 months. During those 8 months, I hit walls I had no idea how to get past. I redesigned components dozens of times. I questioned whether the whole project was even possible given my budget and my tools. There were days where I genuinely didn't know if it would ever work.

What I developed during those 8 months — not by choice but by necessity — was a protocol for being stuck. Not a philosophy. A protocol. A specific sequence of moves for when you're genuinely blocked: simplify the problem, isolate the variable, test one assumption at a time, don't burn out by fighting the whole thing at once.

The 2,000 CAD iterations aren't a number I'm proud of because it's big. I'm proud of it because each iteration was a test. Each one either ruled something out or gave me new information. Failure wasn't the opposite of progress. It was how progress happened.

The Zeeman shift I eventually detected wasn't lucky. It was the result of a thousand smaller decisions to keep going, each one made using this protocol.

That protocol is Module 4. I wrote it down exactly as I used it.
